Introduction

Can a beta be computed?

A beta is the sequence of moves that gets a climber up a route: which hold each hand and foot goes to, and in what order. Climbers work it out by trying. This site works it out by optimization.

Two climbers on the same route often end up with different betas. A tall climber skips a hold that a short climber needs; someone with weak fingers avoids the crimps; someone flexible steps onto a high foot that someone else cannot reach. The aim here is to build a model simple enough to explain in a few chapters, but faithful enough that these differences come out of the math on their own.

Three questions

  • How is it modeled? One chapter per modeling step, from holds and bodies up to the search.
  • What do the results look like? One page per example: a figure climbs the route step by step, with the force on every contact, the effort of every move and the crux marked.
  • Why does the beta differ between climbers? Switch climbers, compare two side by side, and see how the crux changes with height.

The approach in one picture

  1. StatesWhich hold each hand and foot is on. These are the nodes of a graph.
  2. State checkA small convex program: can the climber hold still here, and how hard is the most loaded limb working?
  3. SearchA shortest-path search over moves between states, scored by those efforts.

The best beta is the one whose hardest move, the crux, is as easy as possible. Among all betas with that crux, it is the one with the lowest total cost. Writing \(w(e)\) for the cost of a move \(e\) and \(\mathcal{P}\) for all move sequences from the start to the top:

\[ \text{beta}^\star \;=\; \operatorname*{lex\,min}_{P \in \mathcal{P}} \Big(\, \max_{e \in P} w(e),\;\; \sum_{e \in P} w(e) \Big) \]

Each move cost is built from the effort \(t\) of the state checks it passes through. A state with \(t \le 1\) can be held; \(t > 1\) means some limb would need more force than it has.

How to read this site

The model chapters each follow the same pattern: the idea in climbing terms, the math, an interactive figure, how it is implemented, and the tests that check it. If you only want results, skip to the examples; each one links back to the constraint that decides it. Step 1

Holds, routes and climbers

The inputs: holds, routes, and the four climbers who try them.

The wall

Hold types

A hold is a point with a type. The type fixes the directions it can push a limb in, which strength limit applies, and for hands, how well it holds you in to the wall and resists twisting.

  • \(\theta\), \(\alpha\): the sector; it becomes the direction constraint.
  • Strength limit: which of the climber's limits applies. A sidepull counts as a pinch, an undercling as a crimp.
  • \(\beta\), \(r\) (hands only): the pull toward the wall is at most \(\beta\) times the force along \(\theta\), the twist at most \(r\) times the strength (no tipping, no barn-door). First guesses, open to tuning.

The climbers

  • Strength is in body weights: Forces and limits both scale with weight, so weight itself never matters (a deliberate simplification).
  • A limit of zero rules a hold out before any solving; no crimps also means no underclings.
  • \(\text{flex}\): how far a foot can go above the hip, as a fraction of height \(H\). Negative means below the hip.
  • \(w_{\text{cross}}\): a small style cost for crossing the hands.

Step 2

The body model

A torso that slides but does not turn, with four limbs of fixed greatest length.

Shoulders, hips and limb lengths

The check only needs to know which holds each limb can reach from a given torso position. So the body is a torso \(x = (x_c, y_c)\), which is also the centre of mass, with two shoulders and two hips at fixed offsets (multiples of height \(H\), from standard body proportions; left is negative \(x\)):

What it leaves out

  • No turning: no twisting the hips in for reach.
  • Limbs fold completely: a greatest length but no shortest one.
  • Fixed distance from the wall: It matters for no tipping.
  • Elbows and knees are for drawing only (elbows down and out, knees out).
  • Static moves only: no momentum, no dynos (limits).

Step 3

Checking a state

With every limb on a fixed hold, can the climber hold still, and how hard is the most loaded limb working?

Knowns and unknowns

A state \(\sigma = (\text{LH}, \text{RH}, \text{LF}, \text{RF})\) says which hold each limb is on; a foot may hang. The holds do not fix the body, so the check looks for a body position and forces that work, and the least tiring ones.

\(A\) is the set of limbs on a hold, \(A_F\) those that push, and \(\Sigma_H\), \(\Sigma_F\) sum over the pushing hands and feet. Six constraints, one section each:

The check finds the smallest effort subject to all six, and the state holds if it is at most 1:

\[ t^\star(\sigma) = \min\, t, \qquad t^\star \le 1 \]

A resting limb. During a move one limb lets go (step 4). With no_force=k, limb \(k\) must still reach its hold but carries nothing, so \(A_F = A \setminus \{k\}\).

Output: \(t^\star\), the body position, every force and the limiting limb, or one reason for failing:

    Reach

    Each hold must be within reach of its shoulder or hip, which moves with the body:

    \[ \| p_k - (x + o_k) \|_2 \le L_k, \qquad k \in A \]

    with \(p_k\) the hold, \(o_k\) the shoulder or hip offset and \(L_k\) the limb length. So the body must lie in a disc of radius \(L_k\) around \(p_k - o_k\) for every limb; the overlap of the discs is convex.

    How it is implemented

    Each disc is a second-order cone, used as is. A pair of holds farther apart than both limbs plus the gap between their attachment points is ruled out before solving. Code: stance.py, reach_cones, precheck.

    Direction

    A hold pushes a limb only within its sector: \(f_k\), the force from the hold on the limb, may be at most \(\alpha_k\) from the hold's direction \(d_k = (\cos\theta_k, \sin\theta_k)\):

    \[ d_k^\top f_k \ge \|f_k\|_2 \cos\alpha_k, \qquad k \in A_F \]

    Every \(\alpha_k < 90^\circ\), so this is a convex cone. A foot on a hand hold uses the foothold sector.

    How it is implemented

    In the plane a cone is exactly the space between its two edges: two linear inequalities per limb, \(n_k^{\pm} \cdot f_k \ge 0\). Code: direction_rows.

    Strength and effort

    No limb may exceed its limit \(F_k\) on its hold. The total counts the in-wall force and the pull toward the wall (\(N_k\), hands) or push from it (\(M_k\), feet):

    \[ \begin{aligned} \|(f_k, N_k)\|_2 &\le t\, F_k && \text{hands} \\ \|(f_k, M_k)\|_2 &\le t\, F_{\text{foot}} && \text{feet} \end{aligned} \]

    So \(t\) is the largest share of strength any limb uses, and minimising it moves load off the limb closest to letting go.

    How it is implemented

    One second-order cone per pushing limb, \((t F_k, f_k, N_k)\), used as is. Code: strength_cones, effort.

    In-plane balance

    Seen from the front, the hold forces and gravity \((0, -1)\) at the centre of mass must cancel: no falling or sliding, and no swinging.

    \[ \begin{gathered} \textstyle\sum_{k \in A_F} f_k = (0,\ 1) \\ \textstyle\sum_{k \in A_F} \big( p_{k,x} f_{k,y} - p_{k,y} f_{k,x} \big) = x_c \end{gathered} \]

    Hanging from one hand, that hand carries \(f = (0, 1)\), and the moment equation reads \(p_x = x_c\): the centre of mass must be right below the hand. Moments are taken about the wall origin, where \(p_k\) are known numbers, so the equation stays linear; about the centre of mass it would multiply two unknowns. With zero net force both give the same answer.

    How it is implemented

    Three exact equality rows. Only the most loaded limb is pinned down by the optimal \(t\), so a second solve keeps \(t\) at its optimum and minimises \(\sum |f_{k,x}| + \sum |\tau_k| + \sum N_k + \sum M_k\), dropping squeezes and twists that do nothing. Code: balance_rows, _tidy.

    Out-of-wall balance: no tipping

    The front view alone lets a climber stand on two footholds with idle hands. But the centre of mass is a distance out from the wall, so gravity tips the body back and the hands must pull it in. Treating forces as 3D adds three more balance equations, still linear because the wall is flat and \(d\) is fixed. Here are two; the third is the barn-door.

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{(a)}\ \ & \Sigma_F\, M_k = \Sigma_H\, N_k \\ \text{(b)}\ \ & \Sigma_H\, p_{k,y} N_k - \Sigma_F\, p_{k,y} M_k = d \\ & N_k \le \beta_k\, d_k^\top f_k \ \ \text{(hands)} \end{aligned} \]

    (a): the feet push out as hard as the hands pull in. (b): their moments cancel gravity's. The pull-in is limited by the hold: a sloper (small \(\beta\)) can hardly hold you in. With both feet off, \(d = 0\) and every \(N_k, M_k\) is zero.

    Feet up high, hands work harder. One hand and one foot a height \(h\) apart: (a) gives \(M = N\), then (b) gives \(N = d / h\).

    How it is implemented

    Two equality rows and one inequality per hand, all exact. Earlier figures keep using the constraints of their own sections (the upto option). Code: tipping_rows, wall_distance.

    Out-of-wall balance: no barn-door

    The pulls and pushes must also line up left to right, or they twist the body open about a vertical axis like a door. A hand can resist a little by gripping hard, a twist \(\tau_k\):

    \[ \begin{aligned} \text{(c)}\ \ & \Sigma_H\, p_{k,x} N_k - \Sigma_F\, p_{k,x} M_k + \Sigma_H\, \tau_k = 0 \\ & |\tau_k| \le r_k F_k \end{aligned} \]

    With one hand and two feet, the middle of every hand move, the hand must sit at the push-weighted middle of the feet, give or take what the grip can twist. Both feet on one side and the body swings open.

    How it is implemented

    Flexibility

    A foot cannot go too far above the hip, which moves with the body; a high foot means the body must come up. For every foot on a hold:

    How it is implemented

    One linear inequality on the body height per foot on a hold, a resting foot included. Code: flex_rows.

    The full problem and why it fails

    Convex. The objective is linear, reach and strength are second-order cones, direction is a convex cone, and the rest is linear: a second-order cone program. So \(t^\star\) is unique, and for any bound on \(t\) the workable body positions form a convex set, which makes moves safe (One move).

    • Not strong enough: a solution with \(t^\star > 1\); the report names the most loaded limb and its hold.
    • Cannot reach: no solution, even with only reach and flexibility; the report blames a foot too high for the hip, or else the hold farthest out of reach.
    • Direction or balance: no solution, though reach and flexibility alone can be met.
    • Unusable hold: no strength for that hold type; found before solving.

    Solving it

      How it is implemented

      The program is assembled row by row in Clarabel's standard form (equalities, inequalities, then cones) and solved directly, which skips the modelling overhead of cvxpy. The cross-check writes the same problem straight from the formula above in cvxpy, on random states (four climbers, random hand holds of every type, zero to two footholds, sometimes a resting limb), and the test suite fails if the two ever disagree. Code: run, assemble, reference.py, compare.py.

      Step 4

      One move

      A move changes one limb's hold while the body slides continuously. Two state checks are enough to decide it.

      Five phases of a move

      A state is which hold each limb is on; a body position is where the centre of mass is. A move changes one limb's hold, from state \(\sigma\) to \(\sigma'\), while the body slides. Say the right hand goes from A to B:

      PhaseWhat happensBodyHolds on
      1 Unloadthe force on A drops to zero\(x_0 \to x_1\)\(\sigma\), four limbs
      2 Let goA carries nothing, so the hand can leave\(x_1\)\(\sigma\) hands over to three limbs
      3 Travelthe hand moves to B while the body slides\(x_1 \to x_2\)three limbs
      4 Touchthe hand reaches B, still carrying nothing\(x_2\)three limbs hand over to \(\sigma'\)
      5 Loadthe force on B rises\(x_2 \to x_3\)\(\sigma'\), four limbs

      The two handovers are where the move can fail, and each is one state check with the moving limb resting:

      • Release check check(σ, no_force=k): can the other three hold the body while the hand only touches A? It gives \(x_1\) and \(t_{\text{release}}\).
      • Catch check check(σ′, no_force=k): can they hold it while the hand only touches B? It gives \(x_2\) and \(t_{\text{catch}}\).

      The move works if both are at most 1, and its difficulty is \(\max(t_{\text{release}}, t_{\text{catch}})\). Plain checks of \(\sigma\) and \(\sigma'\) give \(x_0\) and \(x_3\) for the animation only. A foot coming off needs only the release check, a foot going on only the catch check.

      How it is implemented

      Move holds the state, the moving limb and the new hold (or none for a foot coming off) and knows its kind: swap, match, cut or place. evaluate runs the start, release, catch and end checks and reports the difficulty, or which check fails first and why. Code: moves.py.

      Why the feasible region is convex

      The core fact: in one state, the average of two valid solutions is again valid. A solution is a body position \(x\) with every force \(f_k, N_k, M_k, \tau_k\); valid means it meets ① to ⑥ with \(t \le 1\). Mix two of them, \((1-s)\cdot\text{solution}_1 + s\cdot\text{solution}_2\) for \(s \in [0, 1]\):

      ConstraintWhy the mix still meets it
      ① Reachboth positions are in the disc, and a disc has no dents
      ② Directionboth forces are in the sector, which is narrower than 180°
      ③ Strengththe size of a mix is at most the mix of the sizes (triangle inequality)
      ④ ⑤ Balancelinear equations: two solutions equal to the same constant, so is their mix
      ⑤ Pull-in and twist limits, ⑥ Flexibilitylinear inequalities

      So the valid solutions form a convex set, and so do the valid body positions. The effort of the mix is at most \((1-s)\,t_1 + s\,t_2 \le \max(t_1, t_2)\). A climber can slide the body in a straight line between two valid positions while blending the forces, and hold still at every instant.

      Two modelling choices keep this true: moments are taken about the wall origin (about the centre of mass, mixing would break the balance equations), and limbs fold completely (a shortest length would turn each disc into a ring with a hole). It assumes slow, static movement within one state; how the states hand over is the next section. Real bodies, with joint limits, are not convex.

      The exception: swing moves. With no foot pushing, the body hangs flat against the wall (\(d = 0\)); with one, it stands \(d\) off it. Taking off the last foot, or putting on the first, makes the body swing in or out at that moment, so those moves are not straight slides.

        How it is implemented

        For each slide, the two end solutions are packed into vectors of that phase's program and mixed; satisfies checks every equation, inequality and cone. The travel phase uses the three-limb program, with the moving hand neither pushing nor reaching (off). The same check runs on random moves in the test suite. Code: moves.py, segments, path_check; compare.py, path_sweep.
