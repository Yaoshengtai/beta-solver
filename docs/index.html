Introduction

Can a beta be computed?

A beta is the sequence of moves that gets a climber up a route: which hold each hand and foot goes to, and in what order. Climbers work it out by trying. This site works it out by optimization.

Two climbers on the same route often end up with different betas. A tall climber skips a hold that a short climber needs; someone with weak fingers avoids the crimps; someone flexible steps onto a high foot that someone else cannot reach. The aim here is to build a model simple enough to explain in a few chapters, but faithful enough that these differences come out of the math on their own.

Three questions

  • How is it modeled? One chapter per modeling step, from holds and bodies up to the search.
  • What do the results look like? One page per example: a figure climbs the route step by step, with the force on every contact, the effort of every move and the crux marked.
  • Why does the beta differ between climbers? Switch climbers, compare two side by side, and see how the crux changes with height.

The approach in one picture

  1. StatesWhich hold each hand and foot is on. These are the nodes of a graph.
  2. State checkA small linear program: can the climber hold still here, and how hard is the most loaded limb working?
  3. SearchA shortest-path search over moves between states, scored by those efforts.

The best beta is the one whose hardest move, the crux, is as easy as possible. Among all betas with that crux, it is the one with the lowest total cost. Writing \(w(e)\) for the cost of a move \(e\) and \(\mathcal{P}\) for all move sequences from the start to the top:

\[ \text{beta}^\star \;=\; \operatorname*{lex\,min}_{P \in \mathcal{P}} \Big(\, \max_{e \in P} w(e),\;\; \sum_{e \in P} w(e) \Big) \]

Each move cost is built from the effort \(t\) of the state checks it passes through. A state with \(t \le 1\) can be held; \(t > 1\) means some limb would need more force than it has.

How to read this site

The model chapters each follow the same pattern: the idea in climbing terms, the math, an interactive figure, how it is implemented, and the tests that check it. If you only want results, skip to the examples; each one links back to the constraint that decides it. Step 1

Holds, routes and climbers

The inputs: what a hold is, how a route is written down, and the four climbers who try every route.

The wall

Every route is set on the same flat, vertical wall. The figures on this site all use the same marks for walls, holds and climbers, so each mark is introduced here once.

Hold types

A hold is a point on the wall with a type. The type decides three things: the directions in which the hold can push on a hand or foot, which of the climber's strength limits applies, and, for hands, how well the hold lets you pull in toward the wall and resist twisting.

The full parameters of each type:

  • Direction \(\theta\) and half-width \(\alpha\) give the sector above. The force a hold puts on a limb must point inside it; the state check turns this into the direction constraint (Direction).
  • Strength limit says which of the climber's limits applies: the most force one hand can take on that kind of hold, or one foot. A sidepull is held like a pinch and an undercling like a crimp.
  • Pull-in \(\beta\) and grip torque \(r\) only matter for hands. A hand can pull you toward the wall by at most \(\beta\) times the force along \(\theta\), and resist twisting by at most \(r\) times its strength (no tipping, no barn-door). These two values are first guesses and may be tuned later.
  • A foot on a hand hold is treated as if it stood on a : that direction range and the foot's strength.

Route format

A route is a list of holds plus three facts: which two holds the hands start on, which hold is the top, and whether feet may use the hand holds. The climber has to finish with both hands matched on the top hold.

FieldMeaning
id, titleName of the route.

The climbers

Four climbers try every route. Each profile is a handful of numbers: height and arm span, how much force one hand can take on each kind of hold, how high a foot can go, and how much the climber dislikes crossing hands.

  • Strength is measured in body weights: A foot works the same way. Forces and limits both scale with weight, so in this model a climber's weight never matters. That is a deliberate simplification.
  • A sidepull is held like a pinch and an undercling like a crimp (see the table above), so a climber who cannot use crimps cannot use underclings either. A hold with a limit of zero is left out before the state check is even run.
  • Flexibility \(\text{flex}\) is how far a foot can go above the hip, as a fraction of height \(H\). A negative value means the foot has to stay below the hip.
  • Crossing penalty \(w_{\text{cross}}\) is a small extra cost on any move that crosses the hands. It is a matter of style, not physics.

Step 2

The body model

A torso that slides but does not rotate, with four limbs of fixed maximum length.

Shoulders, hips and limb lengths

The state check needs to know one thing about the body: from a given torso position, which holds can each hand and foot reach? So the body is reduced to a torso with four attachment points, two shoulders and two hips, and a greatest length for each limb. The torso position \(x = (x_c, y_c)\) is also the centre of mass. It can slide anywhere on the wall but never turns.

The attachment points sit at fixed offsets from the centre of mass, scaled by the height \(H\) and taken from a standard table of body proportions. Left is negative \(x\):

An arm reaches from the shoulder to the middle of the palm, which is where a hand grips. Half the arm span, minus half the shoulder width and the distance from palm to fingertip, gives the arm length. A leg is half the height:

A foot can also only go so high. With flexibility \(\text{flex}\) from the climber profile, a foot must stay below the hip height plus \(\text{flex}\cdot H\):

Both rules only involve the torso position linearly or through a distance, which is what keeps the state check convex.

What the body model leaves out

  • The torso never turns. Real climbers twist their hips into the wall to gain reach. Here the shoulders and hips keep the same offsets in every position.
  • A limb can fold completely. There is a greatest length but no shortest one, so a hand can be right next to its shoulder. This rarely matters, because the state check prefers spread-out positions anyway.
  • The body is a fixed distance from the wall: This distance matters for the out-of-wall balance (no tipping). Pressing the hips into the wall is not modelled.
  • Elbows and knees are only for drawing. They are placed after the fact so the figure looks like a person: elbows bend down and out, knees bend out like a frog. They never enter the optimisation.
  • Only static moves. There is no momentum, so a move the body cannot hold still at every point is not allowed. Dynos are out of scope (limits).

Step 3

Checking a state

With every limb on a fixed hold, can the climber hold still? If so, how hard is the most loaded limb working?

Knowns and unknowns

A state \(\sigma = (\text{LH}, \text{RH}, \text{LF}, \text{RF})\) says which hold each limb is on. Hands are always on a hold; a foot may hang. The holds alone do not fix the body: the check has to find a body position and forces that work, and the least tiring ones.

Known: the holds and the climber. Unknown:

With \(A\) the limbs on a hold, the unknowns must satisfy six constraints, one section each below:

The check takes the smallest effort; the state can be held if it is at most 1:

\[ t^\star(\sigma) \;=\; \min_{x,\, f,\, N,\, M,\, \tau,\, t} \; t \quad \text{subject to the six constraints}, \qquad \text{hold if } t^\star \le 1 \]

A limb that rests

During a move one limb lets go (step 4). The option no_force=k keeps limb \(k\) within reach of its hold but gives it no force, so the forces and balance only involve \(A_F = A \setminus \{k\}\).

What a check returns

The effort \(t^\star\), the body position, every limb's force, and the limb that limits \(t^\star\). A failed check gives one reason:

    Reach

    The idea. Each hold must be within reach of its shoulder or hip. The shoulders and hips move with the body, so this really limits where the body can be.

    The math. For every limb \(k\) on a hold, with hold position \(p_k\), offset \(o_k\) of its shoulder or hip and limb length \(L_k\):

    \[ \big\| p_k - (x + o_k) \big\|_2 \;\le\; L_k, \qquad k \in A \]

    Seen from the body, each hold must lie inside a circle around its shoulder or hip. Seen from the centre of mass, the positions that reach hold \(k\) form a disc with centre \(p_k - o_k\) and radius \(L_k\); the body can be anywhere in the overlap of the four discs. If they do not overlap, the state is out of reach. An overlap of discs is convex, which the move chapter relies on.

    How it is implemented

    The solver needs linear constraints, so each disc is replaced by the regular polygon with sides inscribed in it: for each direction \(u_i\), \(u_i^\top (p_k - x - o_k) \le L_k \cos(\pi / n)\). The polygon sits inside the circle, so it can only call a reachable hold unreachable, never the reverse, and by at most of the limb length. Before solving, a pair of holds farther apart than the two limbs plus the distance between their attachment points is ruled out at once. Code: stance.py, reach_rows, torso_region, precheck.

    Direction

    The idea. A hold can only push on a hand or foot within a range of directions: a jug holds you from almost anywhere, a sloper only from right below. Here \(f_k\) is the force from the hold on the limb; hanging still, it points up.

    The math. With \(\theta_k\) the hold's direction and \(\alpha_k\) its half-width (step 1), \(f_k\) must lie in the cone

    \[ f_k \in K_k = \big\{\, f \in \mathbb{R}^2 : d_k^\top f \ge \|f\|_2 \cos\alpha_k \,\big\}, \qquad d_k = (\cos\theta_k,\ \sin\theta_k), \qquad k \in A_F \]

    that is, at most \(\alpha_k\) away from \(d_k\). Every \(\alpha_k\) is below 90°, so each \(K_k\) is a convex cone. A foot on a hand hold uses the foothold cone.

    How it is implemented

    In two dimensions a cone is exactly the space between its two edges, so ② becomes two linear inequalities per limb with no approximation: \(n_k^{-} \cdot f_k \ge 0\) and \(n_k^{+} \cdot f_k \ge 0\), with \(n_k^{\pm}\) the inward normals of the edges at \(\theta_k \mp \alpha_k\). Code: stance.py, cone, direction_rows.

    Strength and effort

    The idea. Every hand and foot has a limit: the most force it can take on that kind of hold, from the climber's profile. The effort \(t\) is the share of its limit that the most loaded limb uses. The total counts the in-wall force \(f_k\) and also the part perpendicular to the wall: the hand's pull toward the wall \(N_k\) and the wall's push on a foot \(M_k\), which the out-of-wall balance brings in.

    The math. With \(F_k(\text{type}_k)\) the limit of limb \(k\) on its hold (a sidepull uses the pinch limit, a foot always the foot limit):

    \[ \big\| (f_k,\ N_k) \big\|_2 \le t\, F_k(\text{type}_k) \ \ (\text{hands}), \qquad \big\| (f_k,\ M_k) \big\|_2 \le t\, F_{\text{foot}} \ \ (\text{feet}), \qquad k \in A_F, \qquad t \ge 0 \]

    Every limb's share \(\|(f_k, N_k)\| / F_k\) is at most \(t\), so minimising \(t\) pushes the largest share as low as it will go. That is how a climber settles into a position: shift the weight off whichever limb is closest to letting go.

    How it is implemented

    Code: stance.py, strength_rows, coverage, effort, solve.
