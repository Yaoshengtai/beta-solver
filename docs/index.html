Introduction

Can a beta be computed?

A beta is the sequence of moves that gets a climber up a route: which hold each hand and foot goes to, and in what order. Climbers work it out by trying. This site works it out by optimization.

Two climbers on the same route often end up with different betas. A tall climber skips a hold that a short climber needs; someone with weak fingers avoids the crimps; someone flexible steps onto a high foot that someone else cannot reach. The aim here is to build a model simple enough to explain in a few chapters, but faithful enough that these differences come out of the math on their own.

Three questions

  • How is it modeled? One chapter per modeling step, from holds and bodies up to the search.
  • What do the results look like? One page per example: a figure climbs the route step by step, with the force on every contact, the effort of every move and the crux marked.
  • Why does the beta differ between climbers? Switch climbers, compare two side by side, and see how the crux changes with height.

The approach in one picture

  1. StatesWhich hold each hand and foot is on. These are the nodes of a graph.
  2. State checkA small convex program: can the climber hold still here, and how hard is the most loaded limb working?
  3. SearchA shortest-path search over moves between states, scored by those efforts.

The best beta is the one whose hardest move, the crux, is as easy as possible. Among all betas with that crux, it is the one with the lowest total cost. Writing \(w(e)\) for the cost of a move \(e\) and \(\mathcal{P}\) for all move sequences from the start to the top:

\[ \text{beta}^\star \;=\; \operatorname*{lex\,min}_{P \in \mathcal{P}} \Big(\, \max_{e \in P} w(e),\;\; \sum_{e \in P} w(e) \Big) \]

Each move cost is built from the effort \(t\) of the state checks it passes through. A state with \(t \le 1\) can be held; \(t > 1\) means some limb would need more force than it has.

How to read this site

The model chapters each follow the same pattern: the idea in climbing terms, the math, an interactive figure, how it is implemented, and the tests that check it. If you only want results, skip to the examples; each one links back to the constraint that decides it. Step 1

Holds, routes and climbers

The inputs: holds, routes, and the four climbers who try them.

Holds

Lengths are in metres, forces in body weights. A hold is a point with a type, and the type decides three things: the directions it can push a limb in (the shaded sector), which strength limit applies, and for hands how well it holds you in to the wall (\(\beta\)) and resists twisting (\(r\)).

A sidepull counts as a pinch and an undercling as a crimp. A foot on a hand hold counts as a .

Routes

A route is its holds, the two start holds, and the top hold, which both hands must reach. A campus route also bars feet from hand holds.

Climbers

Strength is in body weights: Weight itself therefore never matters. flex is how far a foot can go above the hip, as a fraction of height. Crossing penalty is a small style cost for crossing the hands. Some examples use variants that change one attribute:

Step 2

The body model

A torso that slides but does not turn, with four limbs of fixed greatest length.

Shoulders, hips and limbs

The body is a point \(x = (x_c, y_c)\), the centre of mass, with An arm reaches from the shoulder to the middle of the palm; a leg is half the height. A foot can go at most \(\text{flex}\cdot H\) above the hip.

Left out: the torso never turns; limbs fold completely; the body stays ; elbows and knees are drawn only; moves are static, no dynos.

Step 3

Checking a state

With every limb on a fixed hold, can the climber hold still, and how hard is the most loaded limb working?

The question

A state says which hold each limb is on; a foot may hang. The holds do not fix the body, so the check looks for a body position and forces that satisfy six constraints, and among those the smallest effort \(t\), the largest share of strength any limb uses. The state holds if \(t \le 1\).

\(A\) is the set of limbs on a hold and \(A_F\) those that push; during a move one limb rests on its hold without force (no_force). \(\Sigma_H\) and \(\Sigma_F\) sum over pushing hands and feet. A failed check gives one reason:

    Reach and flexibility

    Each hold must be within reach of its shoulder or hip, and a foot cannot be too far above the hip. Both limit where the body can be:

    \[ \| p_k - (x + o_k) \|_2 \le L_k \]

    Direction

    A hold pushes a limb only within its sector (the shaded sectors in Holds): the force \(f_k\) from the hold is at most \(\alpha_k\) from its direction \(d_k\). Hanging straight down works on a jug or a sloper; a sidepull or undercling needs another limb pushing back.

    \[ d_k^\top f_k \ge \|f_k\|_2 \cos\alpha_k \]

    Strength and effort

    No limb may exceed its limit \(F_k\) on its hold, counting the in-wall force and the pull toward the wall (\(N_k\), hands) or push from it (\(M_k\), feet):

    \[ \begin{aligned} \|(f_k, N_k)\|_2 &\le t\, F_k && \text{hands} \\ \|(f_k, M_k)\|_2 &\le t\, F_{\text{foot}} && \text{feet} \end{aligned} \]

    Balance

    The forces from the holds must cancel gravity, \((0, -1)\) at the centre of mass, which stands a distance \(d\) out from the wall. A rigid body needs six equations: three in the wall plane (no falling, sliding or swinging) and three out of it (no moving off the wall, tipping back, or swinging open like a door). Moments are taken about the wall origin, so all are linear.

    \[ \begin{aligned} & \textstyle\sum f_k = (0,\ 1), \qquad \sum \big(p_{k,x} f_{k,y} - p_{k,y} f_{k,x}\big) = x_c \\ & \Sigma_F M_k = \Sigma_H N_k, \qquad \Sigma_H\, p_{k,y} N_k - \Sigma_F\, p_{k,y} M_k = d \\ & \Sigma_H\, p_{k,x} N_k - \Sigma_F\, p_{k,x} M_k + \Sigma_H\, \tau_k = 0 \\ & N_k \le \beta_k\, d_k^\top f_k, \qquad |\tau_k| \le r_k F_k \end{aligned} \]

    \(\tau_k\) is how hard a hand twists on its hold. With no foot pushing, the body hangs flat against the wall: \(d = 0\). Three consequences:

    • Hanging from one hand, the centre of mass must be right below it.
    • Feet up high, hands work harder. With one hand and one foot a height \(h\) apart, the hand must pull in \(N = d/h\).
    • Barn-door. With one hand and two feet, the hand must sit between the feet, give or take what the grip can twist.

    The whole problem

    The objective is linear, reach and strength are second-order cones, direction is a convex cone and the rest is linear: a convex second-order cone program. So \(t^\star\) is unique, and for any bound on \(t\) the workable body positions form a convex set, which step 4 relies on. When it fails: a solution with \(t^\star > 1\) means not strong enough; no solution even with only reach and flexibility means cannot reach; otherwise direction or balance does not work.

    Solving it

      Step 4

      One move

      A move changes one limb's hold while the body slides. Two state checks decide it.

      Five phases, two checks

      Say the right hand goes from hold A to hold B, from state \(\sigma\) to \(\sigma'\). The hand unloads A, lets go, travels while the body slides, touches B, and loads it. It can only fail at the two handovers, each a state check with the moving hand resting:

      • Release check check(σ, no_force=k): can the other three hold the body while the hand only touches A?
      • Catch check check(σ′, no_force=k): can they hold it while the hand only touches B?

      The move works if both give \(t \le 1\); its difficulty is \(\max(t_{\text{release}}, t_{\text{catch}})\). A foot coming off needs only the release check, a foot going on only the catch check.

      Why two checks are enough

      In one state, the average of two valid solutions is valid again: discs and sectors have no dents, the size of an average is at most the average of sizes, and the rest is linear. So the body can slide in a straight line between two valid positions, forces blending, with \(t\) never above the larger end.

      Now let \(S_3\) be the body positions where the other three limbs hold the body with \(t \le 1\), and \(D_A\), \(D_B\) where the hand reaches A or B. The release check lands in \(S_3 \cap D_A\), the catch check in \(S_3 \cap D_B\), and in between the hand touches nothing, so the straight slide inside \(S_3\) connects them. Every way of making the move passes both handovers, so the difficulty is exactly \(\max(t_{\text{release}}, t_{\text{catch}})\).

      Exception: taking off the last foot or putting on the first swings the body in or out (\(d\) jumps between 0 and its full value), so those swing moves also need their start and end states to hold.

      Cost and candidates

      A move that works costs its difficulty plus small style terms:

      \[ \begin{aligned} w = {} & \max(t_{\text{release}}, t_{\text{catch}}) + c_0 + \lambda_d\, \|p_B - p_A\|_2 \\ & + w_{\text{cross}}\,[\text{enters crossed hands}] + c_{\text{cut}}\,[\text{a foot comes off}] \end{aligned} \]

      Moves are swaps, matches, cuts (foot off) and places (foot on). Two hands may share a hold, as may two feet; a hand and a foot only if feet may use it; hands never hang. Candidates that fail a cheap reach test are dropped before any full check.

      Step 5

      Finding the best beta

      A two-stage Dijkstra search: first make the crux as easy as possible, then make the rest as cheap as possible.

      The state graph

      The search walks a graph. Nodes are states, edges are moves that work (step 4), weighted by their cost \(w\). Start: both hands on the start holds, the feet in every combination that holds, hanging included. Goal: both hands on the top hold, in a state that holds. Edges are built lazily, only when the search reaches their start state, and every state check is cached.

      How it is implemented

      Two-stage Dijkstra

      The best beta makes the hardest move, the crux, as easy as possible, and among betas with that crux has the smallest total cost. Two searches do this:

      1. Bottleneck Dijkstra. A path's label is the largest move cost on it. Expand states in order of label, starting from every start state at once; the first goal popped has the smallest possible crux \(B^\star\).
      2. Ordinary Dijkstra on the sum of costs, using only moves with \(w \le B^\star\) (plus \(10^{-6}\) for rounding).

      Why not one search with the label \((\text{crux}, \text{total})\) compared in that order? Because the order does not survive extending a path. Take a path with the smaller crux but the larger total, and another with a slightly larger crux but a smaller total. Add a move harder than both, and the cruxes become equal, so the second path now wins. Dijkstra would already have thrown it away. Two stages avoid this. Both share the cached checks, so the second costs almost nothing; the search stops with a "search limit" report after 200,000 expansions.

      No solution: if the first stage runs out of states, the report names the highest state reached and why the next hand move fails, using the diagnosis of step 3, for example “Stuck at step 4 (catch): left hand cannot reach T”.

        How it is implemented

        The tests compare the two-stage search with an exhaustive reference on small routes: for every state and every path length up to a bound, it keeps each (crux, total) pair that no other path beats in both, so the best pair overall is always kept. For Ava, Ben and Cal on an eight-hold route, the two agree exactly on the crux and the total. Code: search.py, bottleneck, cheapest, solve, pareto_reference.

        The beta player

        Smoothing. A state check returns a best body position, but often many are equally good, so frame to frame the body could jump. Every keyframe of the beta (start, release, catch and end of each move) is therefore solved once more: effort at most its own optimum plus , and the body as close as possible to where it was in the previous frame. This changes how the beta looks, never which beta it is.

        Playing a beta. Each move plays its five phases between the four keyframes. Since each slide stays inside one convex region (step 4), every in-between pose is valid too.

          How it is implemented

          Each keyframe is one more cone program: the state's constraints, \(t \le t^\star + \text{slack}\), and the objective \(|x_c - x_c'| + |y_c - y_c'|\) to the previous frame (the first frame aims at a natural height). The run is exported in the format the example pages use: four frames per move, each with the state, body position, forces, shares of strength and joint points. Code: export.py, smooth_frame, export_run.

          Example 1

          Halfway House

          A long gap with one small crimp halfway: reach past it, or hang on it.

          Between the start jugs and the next jug J1 there is a long gap. The only thing in it is a small crimp, C, off to the side, and apart from the start jugs there are no footholds between the floor and the high ones.

          Ben is tall enough to reach J1 straight from the start, with the feet still low (crux 0.63). Ava is not: she pulls onto the crimp with both hands, steps her feet up onto a start jug and a high foothold, and reaches J1 from there (crux 0.71).

          Cal gets a hand onto the crimp but is stuck at move 3, right hand to C, which fails the release check: direction or balance does not work. With one hand on a crimp and both feet to one side, the body needs a little grip twist to stay square, and a weak grip on a small edge cannot give it: a barn-door.

          Ava (injured) cannot use the crimp at all (stuck at move 1, left hand to C, which fails the catch check: a hold this climber cannot use), and without it J1 is out of reach.

            Example 2

            Fork in the Road

            Small crimps straight up, or big jugs far out to the right.

            Two ways up. On the left, three small crimps in a line, with every foothold a few centimetres to their right. On the right, two big jugs, J1 and J2, so far apart that only a long reach links them.

            Ava takes the crimps (crux 0.48). With every foothold off to one side, a hand on a crimp has to twist a little to keep the body square, and her strong fingers can.

            Ben takes the jugs (crux 0.75), using a crimp only as a step while the other hand is on a jug, which does the twisting.

            Cal is stuck at move 3, right hand to C1, which fails the release check: direction or balance does not work. The same twist is too much for a weaker grip, and the body swings open.

            Ava (injured) is stuck at move 1, right hand to C1, which fails the catch check: a hold this climber cannot use. She has Ava's body, so her failure also shows that the jugs alone are too far apart for a climber of Ava's size.

              Example 3

              Lean on Me

              A layback: hands pull sideways on a flake, feet push back, and nothing may twist open.

              A flake runs straight up: four sidepulls, L1 to L4, that pull the hands to the right, with the footholds almost directly below them, a few centimetres to the right. The hands pull, the feet push back, and every time a hand or foot moves, whatever stays on must also stop the body twisting open.

              Ava laybacks up it in 11 moves (crux 0.76).

              Ben gets up too (crux 0.62), but needs 15 moves, with more shuffling. He stands further from the wall, so his hands must pull in harder, and the same small offset of the feet needs more twist.

              Cal gets both hands onto L2 and then cannot move either foot: one foot pushing a few centimetres to the side of two weak sidepull grips swings the body open. With the feet stuck low, Cal is stuck at move 6, left hand to L3, which fails the catch check: out of reach.

              Acceptance checks

                Example 4

                Knees Up

                One high foothold: flexible hips decide, not height.

                Above the start jugs there are only two underclings, and then J1, 0.85 m higher. The footholds are low, except F3, just 25 cm below the start jugs. To reach J1 the feet have to come up, first onto F3 and then onto the start jugs, while the hands pull up on the underclings.

                Ava starts with a foot on F3, a foot near hip height. Her flexible hips allow that. From there she steps onto the jugs and stands up into J1 (9 moves, crux 0.64).

                Ben is stuck at move 7, left hand to J1, which fails the catch check: out of reach. He is much taller, but his stiff hips will not let a foot go anywhere near F3 or the start jugs, and with his feet that low even his reach falls short.

                Ava (less flexible) has Ava's body with Ben's hips. She is stuck at move 6, left hand to J1, which fails the catch check: out of reach.

                Ben (flexible) has Ben's body with Ava's hips, and gets up (7 moves, crux 0.89). So it is the hips, not the height.

                Acceptance checks

                  Example 5

                  Mind the Gap

                  No footholds under the rail: bridge across with a long body, or let the feet go.

                  A traverse to the right along a rail of good jugs, R1 to R4, about 0.47 m apart. The footholds are at both ends, and the wall under the rail is blank for almost 2 m.

                  Ben bridges the gap (10 moves, crux 0.47): with a foot still on the start footholds he can already reach R2, and from there his right foot reaches F3 at the far end. He always has a foot on.

                  Ava is too small for that. She lets both feet go and swings across on her arms, matching each jug (12 moves, crux 0.89): every hand move hangs her whole weight on one jug, t = 1/1.2 = 0.83.

                  Cal can do neither. He is too short to bridge like Ben, and hanging on one jug, like Ava, needs 1/0.8 = 1.25 of his jug strength. The furthest he gets is a stretch from the start to R3 on one foot, and there he is stuck at move 7, left hand to R1, which fails the release check: direction or balance does not work.

                  Acceptance checks

                    Example 6

                    The Stretch

                    How tall do you need to be? One route, one climber, and only the height changes.

                    Low start jugs, one more jug J1 above them, and then nothing: the next jug, J2, is 1.05 m higher, and the only footholds are two chips near the floor. Every climber on this page is the same person at a different height: arm span equal to height, grip and hips halfway between Cal and Ava, and every length of the body in proportion to the height (chapter 2).

                    The chart solves the route at 19 heights from 1.50 m to 1.95 m. Below 1.67 m nobody gets up, and always for the same reason: from J1, J2 is out of reach. From there up, everyone does. At 1.65 m the climber is stuck at move 3, left hand to J2, which fails the catch check: out of reach.

                    Past the threshold, being taller does not make the route easier. The crux even creeps up, from 0.61 at 1.75 m to 0.64 at 1.85 m: a taller body stands further from the wall, so its hands must pull in harder. The tallest climbers save two moves, because they reach the top without stepping onto J1 first. Height buys reach, and on this route reach is all that matters.
